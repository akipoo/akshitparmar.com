# The secret to inevitable success, I guess

*Akshit Parmar · 13 November 2026 · Status: finalised*

Every week, my batch-mates and I share what moved in our businesses. Some of it is small and measured, like a process that finally held for a full month or a number that crept up without anyone pushing it. Some of it is big and emotional, the kind of breakthrough you can hear in someone's voice before they finish the sentence.

I have my own to bring to the table. We are well on our way to becoming a systems-driven company, where my team runs like a tight-knit gang, performance keeps climbing, and more of it runs on auto-pilot every month.

But the stories that make the hair on my arms stand up have nothing to do with systems. They are the relationship breakthroughs, and almost always, they are about a parent.

The most powerful relationship in the world is the one between a parent and a child. Long before we can argue back, we are told what we can and cannot do, and that becomes the floor we build the rest of our lives on. Our parents stay a source of power, inspiration and love long after we stop needing them for anything practical.

What we need from them in return is small. A "well done, beta," a "keep at it," or a "we're proud of you" can carry us for months. Recently, my mom looked at me and said, "Look how far you have come." Do you remember the first time you cleared level one in Mario, with the little flag sliding down the pole and that music playing? That is exactly how it felt.

I was one of the first in our family to start going to the gym, at 18. My daily motivation was simple: come home and show my father my biceps. He would fist bump me, proud, and call me "dude." That loop of effort in the morning and recognition by evening was so strong that years later, when Ayushi asked me why I love the gym so much, this memory was the whole answer.

My grandfather founded the business. It was my father who brought the larger-than-life vision and the execution to match it, and as far back as I can remember, he never held back his praise. Every son wants to hear his parents talk about what he has built with that same pride.

So when my batch-mates from Rahul Jain's program describe that moment with their own parents, a part of me feels fulfilled, almost as if it happened to me.
